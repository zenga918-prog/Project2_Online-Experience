THE
WEIGHT
OF LOVE

A love lock begins as an ordinary object. A name, a pair of initials, or a date is added to a padlock before it is attached to a bridge. The key is often thrown away, turning something made to open and close into a symbol of something that is meant to last.

What gives the lock its meaning is not the object alone, but the emotion placed into it. Sarah Randles describes love locks as emotional objects: things that people use to express, shape, and hold feeling. A small, inexpensive padlock can become intensely personal because it has been connected to a relationship, a memory, or a particular moment. That value is never completely fixed. The same lock can mean different things to different people, and its meaning can change with time.

Place also becomes part of the object. Love locks are often attached to bridges and monuments that already carry their own histories. After the people who placed a lock have left, the object remains behind as a small physical trace of their presence. One lock can seem insignificant, but hundreds begin to change the surface around them. Thousands can alter the structure itself.

This is where the idea of weight becomes complicated. Each lock carries an emotional weight that cannot be measured: a relationship, a promise, a hope, or a memory. Together, however, those gestures acquire literal mass. On the Pont des Arts in Paris, the accumulation of love locks contributed to structural damage and eventually led to changes in the bridge railings.

Removing the locks does not necessarily remove what they have come to represent. Their removal has produced its own emotional reactions, and some locks have been preserved or given new uses rather than simply discarded. Once an ordinary object has been given emotional value, that value can continue after its original location or purpose has changed.

A love lock therefore sits between the personal and the public, between emotion and material. The relationship may change. The place may change. The lock may be removed or left behind. The lock can remain longer than the moment that gave it meaning.

WHAT WE
LEAVE
BEHIND

When I compare photographs of the Pont des Arts before and after its love locks were removed, I notice more than the missing objects. In one image, the locks form a dense surface across the railing. In another, more of the bridge is visible. The difference makes me wonder where the stories attached to those objects have gone. The photographs can show that the locks are no longer there, but they cannot tell us whether the memories have disappeared too.

A love lock is usually placed with the future in mind. Two names and a date connect a small object to a particular relationship. Attaching it to a bridge gives that moment a place outside the people who experienced it. They can leave while the object stays. Part of the appeal seems to come from this difference: people move through a place, but the lock appears to remain. Its continued presence can make a promise feel more secure.

That wish for permanence becomes complicated when the place has other needs. In 2015, Paris replaced the Pont des Arts’ wire-mesh railings with glass panels to prevent more love locks from being attached. The city identifies the accumulated weight of the locks as a cause of damage to the bridge. The change addressed a physical problem, but it also changed a surface that many people had used to represent something personal.

It is easy to understand why someone might feel disappointed if an object connected to their relationship is removed. They may have imagined returning years later and finding it in the same place. Even if they never return, knowing that it is there can become part of the memory. Removal interrupts that imagined future. It reveals how much the meaning of the gesture depends on a place continuing to hold it.

The bridge, however, is also a place that other people use. Someone crossing it every day may care about its condition or the view through its railings. A person responsible for maintaining it has to consider what its structure can support. These concerns exist alongside the feelings attached to individual locks. Looking at the bridge as a shared place makes the decision harder than simply choosing whether the locks are romantic or unwanted.

The scale of the gesture matters. One person leaves one object, but the railing receives all of them. Each visitor can understand their own action as small while seeing the accumulation as something already there. This makes responsibility difficult to locate. No individual lock tells the whole story of the crowded surface. Together, though, the objects change what the bridge looks like and what it has to carry. A personal choice becomes part of a condition shared by many people.

Removal can also begin another part of an object’s life. In May 2017, the City of Paris announced a charity auction of removed love locks. The announcement said that proceeds would go to three organizations supporting refugees. Locks associated with private relationships were being offered a new purpose. Their connection to the bridge became part of a different event, with different people involved.

I find that change more interesting than the idea of an object staying untouched. A lock can carry marks from its first use while entering another situation. It does not arrive without a history, even if a new owner cannot identify the people whose names are written on it. The original story and the later use can exist together. The object’s meaning becomes less certain, but also less limited to the moment when it was first attached.

This is familiar in other objects we keep. A concert ticket no longer gives access to an event once the night is over. A photograph can show a room that has since changed completely. A note can remain important after the relationship around it has changed. Their usefulness and their emotional importance do not end at the same time. Remembering through an object often involves accepting that its surroundings, purpose, or owner will change.

That does not make the loss of a love lock unimportant. A photograph of it may preserve its appearance, but it cannot fully replace knowing that the physical object is still somewhere in the world. Different forms of memory give us different things. The question is how much permanence one form can reasonably promise, especially when it occupies a place that belongs to others as well. A shared bridge cannot remain exactly as every visitor first encountered it.

Thinking about removal has changed the way I understand care in this project. Care can involve preserving an object, but it can also involve repairing a structure, moving something, or allowing a place to be used differently. These actions may disappoint one person while making room for another. The difficulty is acknowledging the emotional meaning of a gesture while taking its material effects seriously. Both are part of what happens after someone leaves a lock behind.

I still understand the wish to give a memory a visible form. It makes a moment easier to point to and return to. But an object cannot keep a relationship or a place from changing. What interests me about love locks is how clearly they reveal that wish, and how their removal brings its limits into view. The lock may leave the bridge while the memory continues elsewhere. What remains can be less visible, and still matter to the people who carry it.

A NAME,
A DATE

Two initials and a date can turn an ordinary padlock into something connected to a particular moment. To someone passing by, the marks may mean very little. To the people who made them, they may hold a whole relationship. I am interested in how so little information can carry so much meaning, even when the story stays private.

THE KEY

A key usually gives us a way back. In the love-lock ritual, it is often the part that gets thrown away. That action makes the promise feel final: the lock is meant to stay closed. But a closed lock cannot decide what happens next. The gesture makes an uncertain future feel certain, at least for the moment.

SIGNS
OF TIME

Scratches, rust, and fading letters make time visible on a lock. The surface changes while the names remain connected to a particular moment. I am interested in this difference between a memory and the object holding it. A lock can look very different years later, even when someone still remembers exactly why they left it there.
